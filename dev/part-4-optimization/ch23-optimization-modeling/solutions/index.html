    Chapter 23 – Solutions¶

    Solutions of the Lab and the Exercises of Chapter 23. Try each task before reading its solution. The code is in the chapter's src folder: lp_duality.py and lp_duality.m (Lab steps 1–2 and Exercise 1, PuLP and Octave glpk), facility_location.py and big_m_study.py (Lab step 3), either_or.py, knapsack_bb.py, search_space.py and knapsack_dp.py (Exercises 2–5). Each module's main() prints every number quoted below; the solver is HiGHS through PuLP 3.

    Lab¶

    1. 2-D LP in GeoGebra (the GeoGebra construction itself is not run here; lp_duality.py checks every number you read off the screen). The feasible region of \(\max 3x_1 + 5x_2\) s.t. \(x_1 \le 4\), \(2x_2 \le 12\), \(3x_1 + 2x_2 \le 18\), \(x \ge 0\) is a pentagon with vertices (0, 0), (4, 0), (4, 3), (0, 6) and (2, 6), where the objective takes 0, 12, 27, 30 and 36 (vertices()). Sliding the level line \(3x_1 + 5x_2 = z\) upward, the last vertex it touches is (2, 6) with \(z = 36\): the optimum of an LP sits at a vertex because the objective is linear, so along any edge it is monotone and the best point is an end of the edge. The slider on the right-hand side 18 (rhs_ranging()): \(z = 30, 33, 36, 39, 42\) for \(b_3 = 12, 15, 18, 21, 24\), one unit of objective per unit of \(b_3\), the shadow price \(y_3 = 1\); it holds for \(12 < b_3 < 24\). Below 12 the vertex moves to the \(x_2\) axis and the price becomes 2.5 (\(x_1 = 0\) and \(2x_2 \le b_3\) decides, worth \(5/2\) per unit); above 24 the constraints \(2x_2 \le 12\) and \(x_1 \le 4\) fix the optimum at (4, 6), \(z = 42\), and the price is 0. At the two ends the vertex is degenerate (three constraints meet) and a solver may report either neighbouring price: HiGHS gives 1 at \(b_3 = 12\) and 0 at \(b_3 = 24\). Rotating the objective (objective_ranging(), \(c_2 = 5\) fixed): the optimum stays at (2, 6) for \(0 < c_1 \le 7.5\) and jumps to (4, 3) beyond; at \(c_1 = 7.5\) the level line is parallel to the edge \(3x_1 + 2x_2 = 18\) and every point of that edge is optimal. At \(c_1 = 0\) the optimum is the whole edge from (0, 6) to (2, 6).
    2. Duality check. solve_lab_lp() builds the LP in PuLP and reads constraint.pi. PuLP hands HiGHS a minimization of \(-c^\top x\), so for a maximization the reported pi are the negatives of the textbook shadow prices; with the sign undone, \(y = (0, 1.5, 1)\). Octave's glpk with sense = -1 returns extra.lambda \(= (0, 1.5, 1)\) directly (lp_duality.m, whose test blocks run under tests/test_octave_sources.py). Complementary slackness: slacks \((2, 0, 0)\) and \(y_k \cdot \text{slack}_k = (0, 0, 0)\): constraint 1 has slack, so its price is zero; constraints 2 and 3 bind, so their prices may be positive. Strong duality: \(b^\top y = 4 \cdot 0 + 12 \cdot 1.5 + 18 \cdot 1 = 36 = z\). By hand, the dual is \(\min 4y_1 + 12y_2 + 18y_3\) s.t. \(y_1 + 3y_3 \ge 3\), \(2y_2 + 2y_3 \ge 5\), \(y \ge 0\); with \(y_1 = 0\) (constraint 1 is slack) and both dual constraints tight (\(x_1, x_2 > 0\)) the two equations give \(y_3 = 1\), \(y_2 = 1.5\).

    3. MILP and big-M. big_m_study.study() draws sites and customers in the unit square (assignment cost 10 times the distance, fixed costs between 3 and 8) and solves four models per instance:

      20 × 60 big-M 79.02 123.36 35.9 % 0.010 0.088 1

Three sentences for the plot (time against size on a log scale, left to the notebook): the strong formulation's relaxation is integral on every instance, so HiGHS proves optimality at the root and the time grows like that of an LP; the big-M relaxation is 29–41 % below the optimum and the solver needs 2 to 4 times longer, the extra work being cuts and presolve in the root node (HiGHS reports a single branch-and-bound node here, so the exponential tree never materializes on instances this small); the lesson is that the formulation, not the solver, decides whether an exact method is cheap, and that on a problem with a weak relaxation and no strengthening available the tree grows exponentially with the number of binaries, which is the situation of optimal transmission switching (Chapter 28).

    Exercise 1¶

    Standard form: replace the free variable by \(x_2 = x_2^+ - x_2^-\) with \(x_2^\pm \ge 0\) and add slacks \(s_1, s_2 \ge 0\):

    \[ \max\ 2x_1 - x_2^+ + x_2^- \quad\text{s.t.}\quad x_1 + x_2^+ - x_2^- + s_1 = 4,\qquad x_1 - x_2^+ + x_2^- + s_2 = 1,\qquad x_1, x_2^\pm, s_1, s_2 \ge 0 . \]

    Dual (one variable per primal constraint, \(y_1, y_2 \ge 0\) because the constraints are \(\le\) in a maximization; one constraint per primal variable, an inequality for \(x_1 \ge 0\) and an equation for the free \(x_2\)):

    \[ \min\ 4y_1 + y_2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_1 + y_2 \ge 2,\qquad y_1 - y_2 = -1,\qquad y \ge 0 . \]

    Graphically (GeoGebra, not run here; exercise_1() solves both with HiGHS): the primal region is the wedge \(x_1 - 1 \le x_2 \le 4 - x_1\), \(x_1 \ge 0\), closed at \(x_1 = 2.5\) where the two lines meet. The objective \(2x_1 - x_2\) prefers small \(x_2\), so along the lower edge \(x_2 = x_1 - 1\) it equals \(x_1 + 1\), largest at the corner (2.5, 1.5): \(z^\ast = 5 - 1.5 = 3.5\). In the dual, \(y_2 = y_1 + 1\) from the equation, the inequality gives \(2y_1 + 1 \ge 2\), i.e. \(y_1 \ge 0.5\), and the objective \(5y_1 + 1\) is smallest at \(y_1 = 0.5\): \(y = (0.5, 1.5)\), value 3.5. The two values agree, as strong duality says, and the two positive dual prices match the two binding primal constraints.

    Exercise 2¶

    With \(y \in \{0, 1\}\): \(x_1 + x_2 \le 4 + M_1 y\) and \(3x_1 + x_2 \le 6 + M_2(1 - y)\); \(y = 0\) enforces the first constraint and relaxes the second, \(y = 1\) the other way round. A big-M is valid when the relaxed constraint excludes no point that the other one allows, so the smallest valid value is the largest violation over the other disjunct, not over the whole box: \(M_1 = \max\{x_1 + x_2 - 4 : 3x_1 + x_2 \le 6,\ 0 \le x \le 10\} = 2\) (at \((0, 6)\)) and \(M_2 = \max\{3x_1 + x_2 - 6 : x_1 + x_2 \le 4,\ 0 \le x \le 10\} = 6\) (at \((4, 0)\)); smallest_valid_m() solves the two small LPs. The box bound alone gives \(M_1 = 20 - 4 = 16\) and \(M_2 = 40 - 6 = 34\), also valid but loose. Smaller values are wrong: \(M_1 = 1\) forbids \((0, 6)\) and \(M_2 = 5\) forbids \((4, 0)\) (test_either_or.py). For \(\max x_1 + x_2\) the integer optimum is 6 at \((0, 6)\), \(y = 1\), for every valid pair. The LP relaxation (\(0 \le y \le 1\)) is where \(M\) matters (solve(..., relax=True)):

    \((M_1, M_2)\) Relaxation bound at \(x\), \(y\)
    (2, 6) 6.00 (0, 6), \(y = 1\)
    (16, 34) 13.37 (3.37, 10), \(y = 0.585\)
    (160, 340) 20.00 (10, 10), \(y = 0.100\)

    The plot of the relaxation's projection onto the \(x\) plane shows why: with a fractional \(y\) both constraints are shifted outward by \(M_1 y\) and \(M_2(1 - y)\), so the relaxed region is the union of the two disjuncts plus the area between them, and the larger \(M\) the more of the box the shifted lines let in; with \(M \to \infty\) the relaxation is the whole box and the bound is useless. With the smallest valid \(M\) the relaxation here is exact (its hull is the convex hull of the two disjuncts), so branch and bound stops at the root; with the box values it starts from a bound more than twice the optimum and has to branch on \(y\).

    Exercise 3¶

    Bound: the LP relaxation of a knapsack is the greedy fractional solution, items in order of value per unit weight \(c\) (3/1), \(a\) (5/2), \(b\) (4/3). branch_and_bound() prints the tree:

    [root]           bound 10.67 = 3 + 5 + 4·(2/3), b fractional -> branch on b
  [b=0]          c = 1, a = 1, weight 3 <= 5: integer, incumbent 8
  [b=1]          capacity left 2: c = 1, a = 1/2 -> bound 9.5 > 8, branch on a
    [b=1, a=0]   c = 1: bound 7 <= 8, pruned by bound
    [b=1, a=1]   weight 5, no room for c: integer, 9 > 8, new incumbent 9

    The optimum is 9 with \(a = b = 1\), \(c = 0\); the first incumbent, 8, came from the greedy choice and was beaten by a solution that leaves out the "best" item \(c\). Four nodes were enough for a tree that could have had \(2^3\) leaves; the pruning of \([b=1, a=0]\) used the bound, that of \([b=1, a=1, c=1]\) never arose because the node was already integer. pulp_check() with HiGHS returns 9 and the same solution.

    Exercise 4¶

    action_counts() draws [52, 39, 32, 18, 20, 5, 7, 3, 13, 50, 40, 55, 32, 38, 59, 45, 39, 34, 35, 57, 19, 50, 41, 3, 25, 52, 35, 4, 47, 45, 52, 13, 8, 53, 4, 34, 7, 20, 30, 27] (sum 1 242). count_workset_size() mirrors ToOp's function: the number of split combinations is \(\sum_{k=0}^{K} \sum_{|S| = k} \prod_{i \in S} n_i\) (the empty set, the unsplit grid, included), and it is multiplied by the number of ways to switch off up to 3 of 200 branches, \(\sum_{d=0}^{3} \binom{200}{d} = 1 + 200 + 19\,900 + 1\,313\,400 = 1\,333\,501\). With at most 2 splits the first factor is \(1 + 1\,242 + \sum_{i<j} n_i n_j = 747\,139\) and the total \(9.96 \times 10^{11}\) candidates: at \(10^5\) evaluations per second, 115 days. With at most 4 splits the product sums reach \(8.1 \times 10^{10}\) and the total \(1.08 \times 10^{17}\): 34 000 years. Even at the \(10^8\) evaluations per second of a GPU batch the four-split enumeration takes 34 years; the space grows with the product of the action counts, i.e. exponentially in the number of simultaneous splits, which is why ToOp samples it (Map-Elites) instead of enumerating it, and why its brute-force generator is a baseline for small cases only.

    Exercise 5¶

    In NP. The decision version asks: is there a subset of items with total weight \(\le W\) and total value \(\ge V\)? A certificate is the subset itself, \(n\) bits. The verifier adds the weights and the values of the chosen items and compares them with \(W\) and \(V\): \(O(n)\) additions of numbers of the input's size, polynomial in the input length. A yes-instance has such a certificate and a no-instance has none, so 0-1 knapsack is in NP (verify_certificate() is the verifier).

    The dynamic program. \(T[i, w]\) = best value using the first \(i\) items with total weight at most \(w\): \(T[0, w] = 0\) and \(T[i, w] = \max(T[i-1, w],\ T[i-1, w - w_i] + v_i)\) when \(w_i \le w\), else \(T[i-1, w]\). The answer is \(T[n, W]\) and the chosen items are read back by walking from \(T[n, W]\) and checking at each row whether the value changed. The table has \(n(W+1)\) cells, each filled in constant time: \(O(nW)\). knapsack_dp() on the instance of Exercise 3 fills 18 cells and returns 9 with items \(\{a, b\}\); for \(n = 50\) the running time goes from 0.3 ms at \(W = 1\,000\) to 1.2 ms at \(W = 10\,000\) and 20 ms at \(W = 100\,000\), linear in \(W\).

    Why this does not contradict NP-completeness. The input writes \(W\) in binary, with \(\log_2 W\) bits (10, 14 and 17 bits in the three runs). A running time of \(O(nW) = O(n\,2^{\text{bits of } W})\) is exponential in the length of the input: doubling the number of bits of \(W\) squares the running time. The algorithm is polynomial only in the numeric value of \(W\), which is what pseudo-polynomial means; a knapsack with weights of 40 bits is beyond this table even for ten items. NP-hardness is a statement about input length, so knapsack is NP-complete and pseudo-polynomially solvable at the same time (it is weakly NP-hard; problems like 3-partition that stay hard with small numbers are strongly NP-hard). The practical reading for topology optimization: an exact method may be fast when the numbers are small or the structure is special, and hopeless on the general case, so one checks the structure before trusting a complexity class in either direction.
