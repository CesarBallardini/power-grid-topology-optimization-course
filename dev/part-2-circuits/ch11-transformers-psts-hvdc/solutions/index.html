    Chapter 11 – Solutions¶

    Solutions of the Lab and the Exercises of Chapter 11. Try each task before reading its solution. The code is in the chapter's src folder: dc_nodal.py (the nodal_solve of the Chapter 8 lab, with susceptances), pst_flows.py (the worked example, lab steps 3 to 5), pst_phasors.py (lab steps 1 and 2, Exercise 5), trafo_equivalents.py and trafo_equivalents.m (Exercises 1 to 3), tap_tables.py (Exercise 4) and hvdc_ring.py (Exercise 6). Each Python module prints every number quoted below when run as a script; the worked examples run live in examples.ipynb.     Lab¶

    1. GeoGebra: quadrature booster against symmetric PST. W_q = U + k i U adds a voltage perpendicular to U, so \(W_q = U(1 + jk)\): its argument is \(\alpha = \arctan k\) and its magnitude \(|U|\sqrt{1 + k^2}\), always larger than \(|U|\). W_s = U (cos(α) + i sin(α)) is \(U e^{j\alpha}\), a pure rotation: abs(W_s) stays at 1 for every k. pst_phasors.quadrature_booster and symmetric_pst give, for \(k = 0.05\), \(0.1\) and \(0.2\), shifts of \(2.862^\circ\), \(5.711^\circ\) and \(11.310^\circ\) with \(|W_q| = 1.0012\), \(1.0050\) and \(1.0198\), and \(|W_s| = 1.0000\) in each case. The symmetric design keeps the magnitude because its series voltage is not perpendicular to the input but to the bisector of input and output: the triangle \(U\), \(W_s\), \(W_s - U\) is isosceles, so the output lies on the same circle as the input. A quadrature booster is cheaper (one series winding fed from a quadrature voltage) and its small magnitude change, second order in \(k\), is accepted. (Construction not run here.)
    2. GeoGebra: the general series voltage. W_g = U + k (cos(φ) + i sin(φ)) with \(U = 1\) has argument \(\operatorname{atan2}(k\sin\varphi,\ 1 + k\cos\varphi)\), the formula of Chapter 1, Exercise 4, and magnitude \(\sqrt{(1 + k\cos\varphi)^2 + (k\sin\varphi)^2}\). pst_phasors.series_voltage(1, 0.05, phi) against phase_shift_deg(0.05, phi): at \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) both give \(2.862^\circ\) with \(|W_g| = 1.0012\) (the quadrature booster of step 1), at \(\varphi = 60^\circ\) both give \(2.419^\circ\) with \(|W_g| = 1.0259\): a step angle below \(90^\circ\) buys less shift and more magnitude change for the same winding. The two values \(2.86^\circ\) and \(2.42^\circ\) are those of Chapter 1 (\(n = 5\) taps of 1 %). (Construction not run here.)

    3. \(F_a\) and \(F_b\) against \(\alpha\). pst_flows.two_path_flows evaluates the closed formula:

      \(\alpha\) \(F_a\) (pu) \(F_b\) (pu)
      \(-10^\circ\) 0.3137 0.6863
      \(-5^\circ\) 0.5318 0.4682
      \(0^\circ\) 0.7500 0.2500
      \(5^\circ\) 0.9682 0.0318
      \(10^\circ\) 1.1863 \(-0.1863\)

      Both lines are straight: \(F_a\) rises by \(0.0436\) pu per degree (slope_per_degree, \((\pi/180)/0.4\)) and \(F_b = P - F_a\) falls by the same amount; the sum is always 1 pu. Line \(b\) carries nothing when \(F_a = P\), that is when \(\theta_{12} = 0\): then \(F_a = \alpha / x_a = P\) gives \(\alpha = x_a P = 0.1\) rad \(= 5.73^\circ\) (alpha_for_zero_flow_b). Beyond it \(F_b\) is negative: the PST pushes more than the whole transfer through line \(a\) and the surplus comes back through line \(b\), a loop flow in the literal sense. At \(10^\circ\) line \(a\) carries 119 % of the transfer. 4. The PST as a pair of injections. With the PST removed, line \(a\) is a plain reactance and the network has injections \(P - \alpha/x_a\) at bus 1 and \(-(P - \alpha/x_a)\) at bus 2; nodal_solve gives \(\theta_{12} = (P - \alpha/x_a) / (1/x_a + 1/x_b)\) and the flows \(\theta_{12}/x_a\), \(\theta_{12}/x_b\). Adding \(\alpha/x_a\) back to line \(a\) gives \((\theta_{12} + \alpha)/x_a\), the PST branch's true flow:

      pst_flows.py (injections and PSDF)
      def two_path_by_injections(alpha_deg: float, p: float = P, x_a: float = X_A, x_b: float = X_B) -> tuple:
    """F_a and F_b from ``nodal_solve`` with the PST replaced by injections −α/x_a at bus 1 and +α/x_a at bus 2.

    The plain two-line network is solved with the modified injections; the PST's own branch then gets α/x_a
    added back, because the angle across its reactance is θ12 + α, not θ12.
    """
    shift = np.radians(alpha_deg) / x_a
    inj = np.array([p - shift, -p + shift])
    _, flows = nodal_solve([0, 0], [1, 1], [1 / x_a, 1 / x_b], inj, 2)
    return flows[0] + shift, flows[1]


def psdf_column(
    fr: np.ndarray, to: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, pst_branch: int, inj: np.ndarray, n: int, step_deg: float = 1.0
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Change of every branch flow per degree of a PST on ``pst_branch``, by finite differences.

    The DC model is linear, so the difference quotient is exact whatever the step and the injections; the
    column is what ToOp's PSDF stores for that transformer (before its base_mva × π/180 scaling).
    """
    shift = np.radians(step_deg) * b[pst_branch]
    inj_pst = np.array(inj, dtype=float)
    inj_pst[fr[pst_branch]] -= shift
    inj_pst[to[pst_branch]] += shift
    _, base = nodal_solve(fr, to, b, inj, n)
    _, shifted = nodal_solve(fr, to, b, inj_pst, n)
    shifted[pst_branch] += shift
    return (shifted - base) / step_deg

      Open the src/ folder on GitHub to see or download all the files of this chapter.

      two_path_by_injections agrees with two_path_flows to \(10^{-12}\) at every angle tested (the test runs \(-10^\circ\), \(-3^\circ\), \(0^\circ\), \(4.5^\circ\) and \(10^\circ\)). The equivalence is exact in the DC model because the PST equation \(F_a = (\theta_{12} + \alpha)/x_a\) is linear: the term \(\alpha/x_a\) is a constant flow entering bus 2 and leaving bus 1 through branch \(a\), and a constant flow through a branch is indistinguishable, for the rest of the network, from an injection pair at its ends. This is why ToOp can keep one nodal matrix for all taps and move the PST angle into the injection vector (dc_solver/jax/pst.py). 5. A PSDF column. psdf_column(FR, TO, B, 3, INJ, 6) puts the PST on branch (1, 3) of the Chapter 2 network (branch index 3) and takes the difference quotient of the flows for a step of one degree:

      Branch base flow (pu) change per degree (pu)
      (0, 1) 0.3041 \(+0.00612\)
      (0, 2) 0.1959 \(-0.00612\)
      (1, 2) 0.0702 \(-0.01470\)
      (1, 3) 0.2339 \(+0.02082\)
      (2, 4) 0.7661 \(-0.02082\)
      (3, 4) 0.2339 \(+0.02082\)
      (4, 5) 1.0000 \(0\)

      The column is a circulation: it adds up to zero at every node (test_psdf_column_is_exact_and_step_independent checks \(A^\top \Delta F = 0\)), so it changes no injection, only the split of the flows among parallel paths. The PST pushes \(0.0208\) pu per degree through its own branch and on through (3, 4); the same amount returns through (2, 4), then back to node 1 partly through (1, 2) (\(0.0147\)) and partly around node 0 (\(0.0061\) through (0, 2) and (0, 1)). Branch (4, 5) does not react: node 5 is a leaf, so KCL fixes its flow at the 1 pu withdrawn there, whatever any PST does. The column is the same for a step of \(0.01^\circ\) and the same for zero injections (the test checks both), which is the point: in the linear DC model the sensitivity does not depend on the operating point, so ToOp computes it once in preprocessing (dc_solver/preprocess/helpers/psdf.py) and scales it by base_mva * (np.pi / 180) to get MW per degree, \(2.08\) MW per degree on 100 MVA for branch (1, 3) here.

    Exercise 1¶

    An impedance \(Z\) on the low-voltage side draws \(I_2 = V_2 / Z\); the ideal transformer carries \(V_1 = (380/110) V_2\) and \(I_1 = (110/380) I_2\), so from the high-voltage side

    \[ Z' = \frac{V_1}{I_1} = \left(\frac{380}{110}\right)^2 \frac{V_2}{I_2} = \left(\frac{380}{110}\right)^2 Z = 11.934 \times 10 = 119.3\ \Omega \]

    (trafo_equivalents.referred_impedance(10, 380, 110) gives \(119.34\ \Omega\)). Impedances scale with the square of the ratio because voltage goes up by the ratio and current down by it. This is also why per-unit values do not depend on the side (Chapter 10, Exercise 4): the base impedance scales by the same square.

    Exercise 2¶

    The DC susceptance of a branch is \(b = 1/x\). Replacing \(x\) by \(x/\rho\) gives \(b' = \rho / x = \rho\, b\), a change of \(\rho - 1 = +5\) % for \(\rho = 1.05\) (susceptance_change). A larger susceptance means a stiffer branch, so in a meshed network the transformer attracts more flow: in the two-path example with the transformer (\(x = 0.1\)) in parallel with a line (\(x = 0.3\)), its share of a 1 pu transfer rises from \(0.7500\) to \(0.7590\) (flow_share_with_ratio), a change of \(+1.2\) %, less than 5 % because the parallel path dilutes it: \(\rho b_t / (\rho b_t + b_\ell)\) against \(b_t / (b_t + b_\ell)\). A \(\rho\) below 1 would do the opposite. The sign of the effect depends on which side the reactance is referred to: PowSyBl's dc_use_transformer_ratio = True and ToOp's get_trafos make the same choice, which is what the comment in the code records.

    Exercise 3¶

    \[ x_1 = \frac{0.10 + 0.25 - 0.20}{2} = 0.075, \qquad x_2 = \frac{0.10 + 0.20 - 0.25}{2} = 0.025, \qquad x_3 = \frac{0.25 + 0.20 - 0.10}{2} = 0.175\ \text{pu}, \]

    and indeed \(x_1 + x_2 = x_{12}\), \(x_1 + x_3 = x_{13}\), \(x_2 + x_3 = x_{23}\): each pairwise short-circuit test sees the two star branches in series, which is where the formula comes from (trafo_equivalents.star_equivalent; the Octave version is trafo_equivalents.m). A star branch can come out negative in real units when one winding sits between the other two; it is still a valid branch of the equivalent, only not a physical reactance.

    The star node is not a busbar: it is a fictitious point inside one tank. A fault or a maintenance outage of the transformer removes the whole unit, so the three branches that meet at the star node disappear together, and leaving the star node connected to one of them would create a dangling node with no injection. That is why the pandapower backend turns the outage of a three-winding transformer into a multi-outage of three branches (get_trafo3w_multioutage), and why the PowSyBl backend simply refuses networks that contain one.

    Exercise 4¶

    tap_tables.constant_x_table() builds taps \(-16 \dots 16\) with \(x = 0.1\) pu throughout and \(\alpha(n) = \operatorname{atan2}(0.01 n \sin 90^\circ,\ 1 + 0.01 n \cos 90^\circ) = \arctan(0.01 n)\); growing_x_table() keeps the same \(\alpha\) and sets \(x = 0.1\,(1 + 0.01 |n|)\), \(0.116\) pu at the end taps. is_linear_pst applies np.allclose(x, x[0]): True for the first table, False for the second. ToOp treats the first as linear in DC (one PSDF column, the angle in the injection vector) and needs per-tap susceptances for the second, as the chapter's ToOp note says.

    \(\alpha\) is not exactly proportional to the tap number in the first table: \(\arctan\) grows more slowly than its argument. The shift per tap is \(0.5729^\circ\) at tap 1 and only \(0.5681^\circ\) at tap 16, where \(\alpha = 9.090^\circ\) against \(16 \times 0.5729^\circ = 9.167^\circ\) if it were proportional, a shortfall of \(0.08^\circ\) (0.8 %). It is proportional to third order, \(\alpha \approx 0.01 n - (0.01 n)^3/3\), which is why the first few taps look linear and the end taps do not. Since ToOp reads the angle of the selected tap from a table instead of multiplying a step by a tap number, this non-linearity costs it nothing; "linear" in get_linear_pst refers to the reactance, not to the angle.

    Exercise 6¶

    hvdc_ring.py uses the ring \(x_{12} = 0.10\), \(x_{23} = 0.15\), \(x_{13} = 0.20\) pu with a generator at bus 1 feeding loads of 0.6 pu at bus 2 and 0.4 pu at bus 3. The HVDC link is inj[0] -= p_dc, inj[2] += p_dc before nodal_solve (bus 1 is the slack, so the withdrawal at bus 1 is absorbed by the balance anyway). At \(P_{dc} = 0\) the AC flows are \(0.6444\) pu on L1-2, \(0.0444\) on L2-3 and \(0.3556\) on L1-3, and L1-2 is the most loaded line. Because the DC model is linear, \(F(P_{dc}) = F(0) + P_{dc}\, d\) with \(d = F(1) - F(0) = (-0.4444, -0.4444, -0.5556)\): every unit the link carries is a unit that no longer travels over the AC ring, \(0.444\) of it along the path 1–2–3 and \(0.556\) along L1-3, in inverse proportion to the path reactances (\(0.25\) and \(0.20\)). The test checks the affine law at three setpoints and the script at nine: the largest deviation is \(2 \times 10^{-16}\).

    Unloading the most loaded line can mean two things. The flow on L1-2 reaches zero at \(P_{dc} = 0.6444/0.4444 = 1.45\) pu, but by then L1-3 carries \(-0.45\) pu backwards, so the line that was relieved has handed the problem to its neighbour. The sensible target is the setpoint that minimizes the largest AC flow, a one-variable linear program (best_setpoint, solved with scipy.optimize.linprog): \(P_{dc} = 0.775\) pu, where L1-2 carries \(+0.300\) pu, L2-3 \(-0.300\) pu and L1-3 \(-0.075\) pu. The maximum AC loading falls from \(0.644\) to \(0.300\) pu, and moving the setpoint \(0.1\) pu either way makes it worse again (the test checks both sides). The figure in examples.ipynb shows the three straight lines crossing and the setpoint where \(|F_{12}| = |F_{23}|\).

    The HVDC setpoint is a continuous action: \(P_{dc}\) can take any value between the converter limits and every flow responds linearly and smoothly, so the optimization is a small linear program with a unique, interior optimum, as above. A busbar split is a discrete choice among configurations (Chapter 19 counted them): there is no "half a split", the flows jump from one topology to the next, and the problem becomes combinatorial. That difference is why ToOp's action-set schema reserves HVDCRange with min_power and max_power for the continuous variable, even though the solver does not optimize it yet, while busbar splits and PST taps are enumerated.
