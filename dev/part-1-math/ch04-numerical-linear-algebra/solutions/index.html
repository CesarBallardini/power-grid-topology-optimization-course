    Chapter 4 – Solutions¶

    Solutions of the Lab and the Exercises of Chapter 4. Try each task before reading its solution. The code is in the chapter's src folder: sherman_morrison.py (the network and the rank-1 update of the worked example), rank_one_lab.py (lab steps 1–3), near_bridge.py (lab step 4 and Exercise 6), woodbury.py (Exercise 3), floating_point.py (Exercise 4), sparse_fill_in.py and fill_in.m (Exercise 5). Each module's main() prints every number quoted below; the worked examples run in examples.ipynb. Timings are from one run on the maintainer's laptop and will differ on yours; the ratios are what matters.

    Lab¶

    1. Denominators. With \(G_r^{-1}\) computed once, denominator_table evaluates \(g_k a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k\) for the seven branches (node 0 grounded):

      Branch Ends \(g_k\) (S) \(g_k R_{\text{eff},k}\) \(1 - g_k R_{\text{eff},k}\)
      0 0–1 10 0.754386 0.245614
      1 0–2 5 0.508772 0.491228
      2 1–2 8 0.631579 0.368421
      3 1–3 4 0.701754 0.298246
      4 2–4 6 0.801170 0.198830
      5 3–4 3 0.602339 0.397661
      6 4–5 2 1.000000 0.000000

      Branch 6, (4, 5), is the only one with a zero denominator (to \(10^{-12}\)). It is the only branch at node 5, so its removal leaves node 5 with no path to ground: the reduced matrix \(G_r'\) gets a zero row and column and is singular, and the Sherman–Morrison update divides by zero. Every other value lies strictly between 0 and 1, as Exercise 2 proves it must. 2. Three ways to remove (1, 3). With 1 A injected at node 5, the voltages of nodes 1–5 before the removal are \((0.054971, 0.090058, 0.122222, 0.211891, 0.711891)\) V and after it \((0.047059, 0.105882, 0.272549, 0.272549, 0.772549)\) V, whether \(G_r'\) is rebuilt and solved, the inverse is updated by Sherman–Morrison, or the solution is updated directly with \(v' = v + G_r^{-1} a_k\, \dfrac{g_k\, a_k^\top v}{1 - g_k a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k}\) (remove_three_ways and update_solution). The three answers agree to \(5.6 \times 10^{-17}\), round-off of float64. Node 3 now hangs off node 4 through branch (3, 4) alone and carries no current, so \(v_3 = v_4\); node 5 rises because the current has lost one of its two paths from node 4 to ground. The direct update is the cheapest of the three: the branch's old flow \(g_k a_k^\top v\) is re-injected at its ends and spread with the old inverse, one matrix-vector product and no new matrix. 3. Timings on the 40 × 40 grid. The grid has 1 600 nodes and 3 120 branches with random conductances in \([0.5, 2)\) S. For 100 random single-branch removals (time_removals), rebuilding the sparse \(G_r'\) and solving it with spsolve took 0.338 s in total, against 0.026 s for one splu factorization followed by 100 updates of the solution, in which \(G_r^{-1} a_k\) is obtained as lu.solve(a_k) (two triangular solves) and no inverse is ever formed: 12.9 times faster, with a largest disagreement between the two methods of \(2.4 \times 10^{-13}\). plot_timings shows two straight lines: the update line starts at the one-off factorization cost and has a much smaller slope, so it wins after the first few removals. This is why ToOp factors once (spsolve in compute_ptdf) and then applies low-rank updates on the GPU for every topology it evaluates. 4. Near-bridge in float32 and float64. With the weak branch (5, 0) added, (4, 5) has a parallel path and its denominator becomes positive but small: about \(g_w / 2\), because the rest of the network between nodes 4 and 5 has conductance \(\approx g_w\) and \(1 - g_k R_{\text{eff}} = g_{\text{rest}} / (g_k + g_{\text{rest}})\). After the removal, node 5 is tied to ground only through \(g_w\), so its voltage is exactly \(1 / g_w\) (precision_table):

      \(g_w\) (S) Denominator \(v_5\) re-solved \(v_5\) float32 rel. error float32 rel. error float64
      \(10^{-2}\) \(4.965 \times 10^{-3}\) 100 100.00 \(1.1 \times 10^{-5}\) \(1.9 \times 10^{-14}\)
      \(10^{-4}\) \(5.0 \times 10^{-5}\) \(10^{4}\) \(1.001 \times 10^{4}\) \(9.6 \times 10^{-4}\) \(1.9 \times 10^{-12}\)
      \(10^{-6}\) \(5.0 \times 10^{-7}\) \(10^{6}\) \(1.049 \times 10^{6}\) \(4.9 \times 10^{-2}\) \(1.6 \times 10^{-10}\)

      The denominator is a difference of two numbers close to 1, so its absolute error is about the machine epsilon of the format (\(1.2 \times 10^{-7}\) for float32) and its relative error is \(\varepsilon / \text{denominator}\): \(2 \times 10^{-5}\), \(2 \times 10^{-3}\) and \(0.2\) in float32 for the three values, which is the order of the errors observed in \(v_5\). In float64 (\(\varepsilon = 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\)) the same ratio stays below \(10^{-9}\). ToOp evaluates exactly this kind of update for every contingency and every split, with denominators that can be as small as the weakest parallel path allows; float32 would turn near-bridges into answers wrong by percent, which is why jax_enable_x64 is forced on. Its 1e-11 threshold (jnp.abs(denom) > 1e-11 in dc_solver/jax/lodf.py) sits about four orders of magnitude above float64 round-off: a denominator below it is indistinguishable from the exact zero of a bridge and is treated as islanding rather than divided by.

    Exercise 1¶

    Let \(\beta = v^\top M^{-1} u\), a scalar, and multiply:

    \[ (M + u v^\top)\left(M^{-1} - \frac{M^{-1} u v^\top M^{-1}}{1 + \beta}\right) = I - \frac{u v^\top M^{-1}}{1 + \beta} + u v^\top M^{-1} - \frac{u\,(v^\top M^{-1} u)\, v^\top M^{-1}}{1 + \beta}. \]

    The last term contains the scalar \(\beta\), so the three terms in \(u v^\top M^{-1}\) collect to

    \[ u v^\top M^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + \beta} - \frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\right) = u v^\top M^{-1} \cdot 0, \]

    and the product is \(I\). The right-hand side is a right inverse of \(M + u v^\top\), hence the inverse.

    When \(1 + \beta = 0\) the formula divides by zero, and rightly so: \((M + u v^\top)\, M^{-1} u = u + u\,\beta = (1 + \beta)\, u = 0\) with \(M^{-1} u \ne 0\) (as \(u \ne 0\)), so \(M + u v^\top\) has a non-trivial null vector and is singular. There is no inverse to compute. In the resistor network, \(u = -g_k a_k\) and \(v = a_k\), so \(1 + \beta = 1 - g_k a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k\), and the singular case is the bridge of lab step 1 (bridge_indicator returns 0 for branch 6, and remove_branch raises ZeroDivisionError). sherman_morrison.py's test test_rank_one_update_matches_fresh_inverse is the numerical version of this identity for the six non-bridge branches.

    Exercise 2¶

    Write \(G_r = G_{\text{rest}} + g_k a_k a_k^\top\), where \(G_{\text{rest}}\) is the reduced Laplacian of the network without branch \(k\).

    Circuit argument. \(a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k\) is the effective resistance \(R_{\text{eff},k}\) between the two ends of branch \(k\) (if one end is the grounded node, between the other end and ground). Between those two terminals the network is branch \(k\), of resistance \(r_k = 1/g_k\), in parallel with the rest of the network, of effective resistance \(R_{\text{rest}} \in (0, \infty]\): finite when the rest still joins the two ends, infinite when it does not. The parallel rule gives

    \[ R_{\text{eff},k} = \frac{r_k R_{\text{rest}}}{r_k + R_{\text{rest}}} \le r_k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad g_k R_{\text{eff},k} = \frac{R_{\text{rest}}}{r_k + R_{\text{rest}}} \le 1, \]

    with equality exactly when \(R_{\text{rest}} = \infty\), that is, when no other path joins the ends of branch \(k\), which is the definition of a bridge (the ground counts as a node: a bridge to the grounded node islands the far side from ground).

    Algebraic argument. If \(G_{\text{rest}}\) is invertible, Sherman–Morrison applied to \(G_r = G_{\text{rest}} + g_k a_k a_k^\top\) gives, with \(x = g_k\, a_k^\top G_{\text{rest}}^{-1} a_k \ge 0\) (positive definiteness),

    \[ g_k\, a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k = x - \frac{x^2}{1 + x} = \frac{x}{1 + x} < 1 . \]

    If \(G_{\text{rest}}\) is singular, removing branch \(k\) has islanded a set \(S\) of ungrounded nodes; let \(z\) be its indicator vector, so \(G_{\text{rest}} z = 0\) and \(a_k^\top z = \pm 1\) (exactly one end of \(k\) is in \(S\)). Then \(G_r z = g_k a_k (a_k^\top z) = \pm g_k a_k\), so \(G_r^{-1} a_k = \pm z / g_k\) and \(g_k a_k^\top G_r^{-1} a_k = (a_k^\top z)^2 = 1\). Thus \(g_k R_{\text{eff},k} = 1\) if and only if branch \(k\) is a bridge, and the denominator \(1 - g_k R_{\text{eff},k}\) of the worked example vanishes exactly on bridges. Lab step 1 shows the strict inequality on the six other branches, with values from 0.51 to 0.80.

    Exercise 3¶

    Removing branches \(j\) and \(k\) changes the reduced Laplacian by \(-U C U^\top\) with \(U = [a_j\ a_k]\) and \(C = \operatorname{diag}(g_j, g_k)\). Woodbury with \(M = G_r\) and \(V = U\) gives

    \[ (G_r - U C U^\top)^{-1} = G_r^{-1} + G_r^{-1} U \left(C^{-1} - U^\top G_r^{-1} U\right)^{-1} U^\top G_r^{-1}, \]

    so the only new system is the \(2 \times 2\) one with matrix \(C^{-1} - U^\top G_r^{-1} U\) and right-hand side \(U^\top G_r^{-1}\) (two rows). solve2x2 in woodbury.py solves it in closed form, adjugate over determinant, and returns the solution with a Boolean flag |det| > 1e-10; remove_two_branches assembles the update. For branches (1, 2) and (3, 4) the result matches the inverse rebuilt without them to \(1.7 \times 10^{-16}\), and for all 11 pairs of non-bridge branches that leave the network connected the test passes at \(10^{-12}\). Two successive rank-1 updates give the same matrix as one rank-2 update.

    Four pairs are singular: (0, 1) with (0, 2) cuts the whole network from ground, (1, 3) with (2, 4) cuts nodes 3–5 off, (1, 3) with (3, 4) isolates node 3 and (2, 4) with (3, 4) isolates nodes 4 and 5. For the last pair the \(2 \times 2\) matrix is

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} 0.033138 & -0.066277 \\ -0.066277 & 0.132554 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = 9.2 \times 10^{-19}, \]

    whose second row is \(-2\) times the first: the two branches are the only connections of the island, so their flows after removal are not independent. The flag is False and the "solution" (infinities) is to be ignored. Any pair that includes the bridge (4, 5) is singular too.

    ToOp's solve2x2 (dc_solver/jax/unrolled_linalg.py) is the same code in JAX: det = m1 * m4 - m2 * m3, a_inv = jnp.array([[m4, -m2], [-m3, m1]]) * (1.0 / det), res = jnp.dot(a_inv, b), and it returns (res, jnp.abs(det) > 1e-10). It never raises, because it runs vectorized over thousands of topologies on the GPU, where an exception in one lane is not an option: the Boolean flag travels with the result and the caller masks out the islanding cases. solve3x3 does the same for the \(3 \times 3\) case, which is why the chapter says ToOp uses "closed-form 2×2/3×3 inverses plus determinant checks".

    Exercise 4¶

    np.finfo (finfo_table):

    float32 float64
    mantissa bits 23 52
    machine epsilon \(1.19 \times 10^{-7}\) \(2.22 \times 10^{-16}\)
    decimal digits 6 15
    smallest normal \(1.18 \times 10^{-38}\) \(2.23 \times 10^{-308}\)
    largest \(3.40 \times 10^{38}\) \(1.80 \times 10^{308}\)

    The three expressions (surprises):

    • 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is False: none of the three decimals is representable in binary, and the rounded sum is 0.30000000000000004, one unit in the last place above the rounded 0.3.
    • 1e16 + 1 - 1e16 is 0.0: near \(10^{16}\) the spacing of float64 numbers is 2, so adding 1 rounds back to \(10^{16}\) before the subtraction. One decade lower, 1e15 + 1 - 1e15 is 1.0.
    • np.sum([1.0, np.nan]) is nan: NaN is absorbing. nan_spreads shows a single NaN entry turning a sum, a mean and a max into NaN and poisoning every entry of a dense matrix-vector product.

    compute_ptdf (dc_solver/preprocess/helpers/ptdf.py) ends with assert np.isnan(ptdf).sum() == 0 because the PTDF is obtained from a sparse solve with the node susceptance matrix: if the grid is not connected the matrix is singular and the solver returns infinities or NaNs instead of raising; if some susceptance is NaN, very small or very large, the same happens. Once a NaN is in the PTDF, every later product (LODF, flows, metrics for every topology) is NaN too, silently, and an optimizer comparing NaN objectives makes arbitrary choices. Checking once at the source, where the message can still say what went wrong, is far cheaper than finding the poison downstream. The assertion's message names exactly these causes.

    Exercise 5¶

    fill_in_table builds \(G_r\) of the 40 × 40 grid as CSC (1 599 × 1 599, 7 835 nonzeros: the diagonal plus two entries per branch not at the grounded node) and factors it with splu:

    Ordering nnz(L) + nnz(U) Fill ratio
    \(G_r\) itself 7 835 1.00
    NATURAL 126 319 16.1
    COLAMD 63 291 8.1
    MMD_AT_PLUS_A 38 325 4.9

    (The unit diagonal of \(L\) is not counted.) In the natural ordering the grid's bandwidth of 40 fills the whole band during elimination: the factors are 16 times denser than the matrix. COLAMD halves that. The third row, not asked for, is SuperLU's ordering for matrices with a symmetric pattern and does better still; COLAMD is designed for unsymmetric matrices and does not exploit the symmetry of \(G_r\).

    Octave (fill_in.m, same grid, Cholesky instead of LU): nnz(G) is 7 835, nnz(chol(G)) is 63 959 (8.2 times the matrix) and, after p = symamd(G), nnz(chol(G(p, p))) is 19 955 (2.5 times). A Cholesky factor is one triangle, so compare it with half of \(L + U\); symamd lands near SuperLU's symmetric ordering. spy(G), spy(chol(G)) and spy(chol(G(p, p))) show the banded matrix, the filled band and the sparse scattered factor. For the 10 × 10 grid the three counts are 455, 989 and 601. The lesson for ToOp: spsolve on the node susceptance matrix pays this fill-in once; the GPU updates that follow touch only dense small blocks and never refactor.

    Exercise 6¶

    The matrix before the removal is the flat curve: condition_numbers(weak) gives \(\kappa_2(G_r)\) from 25.83 at \(g_w = 10^{-2}\) to 26.00 at \(10^{-6}\), the value of the network without the weak branch, with a log–log slope of \(0.000\). While branch (4, 5) is present, node 5 is well connected and the matrix does not care about the tiny extra conductance. The ill-conditioned one is \(G_r'\), the matrix after removing (4, 5), whose smallest eigenvalue is about \(g_w\) (node 5 hanging on the weak branch). condition_numbers(weak, removed=True) gives

    \(g_w\) (S) \(\kappa_2(G_r)\) \(\kappa_2(G_r')\) float32 rel. error of \(v_5\)
    \(10^{-2}\) 25.83 \(2.99 \times 10^{3}\) \(1.1 \times 10^{-5}\)
    \(10^{-3}\) 25.98 \(2.99 \times 10^{4}\) \(3.2 \times 10^{-4}\)
    \(10^{-4}\) 25.99 \(2.99 \times 10^{5}\) \(9.6 \times 10^{-4}\)
    \(10^{-5}\) 26.00 \(2.99 \times 10^{6}\) \(1.4 \times 10^{-3}\)
    \(10^{-6}\) 26.00 \(2.99 \times 10^{7}\) \(4.9 \times 10^{-2}\)

    The log–log slope of \(\kappa_2(G_r')\) is \(-1.000\): the condition number is inversely proportional to the weak conductance. The float32 error has a slope of about \(-0.9\) over nine points between \(10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-2}\) (it is noisy, since round-off depends on the particular bits of each value), consistent with the rule of thumb "relative error \(\approx \kappa\, \varepsilon\)": at \(g_w = 10^{-6}\), \(\kappa_2(G_r') \varepsilon_{32} = 3 \times 10^{7} \times 1.2 \times 10^{-7} \approx 3.6\), so float32 has no correct digits left in the worst direction, and the 5 % error in \(v_5\) is a lucky component. With float64, \(\kappa \varepsilon_{64} \approx 7 \times 10^{-9}\) and the observed \(1.6 \times 10^{-10}\) is comfortably inside. The Sherman–Morrison denominator is the bridge between the two views: \(1 - g_k R_{\text{eff}} \approx g_w / 2\) shrinks in step with the smallest eigenvalue of \(G_r'\), so a small denominator and a large \(\kappa_2(G_r')\) are the same warning. plot_condition draws the three curves of the table on log–log axes.
